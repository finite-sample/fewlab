\title{Estimand-Driven Sampling of Reusable Labels}
\author{Gaurav Sood}

\begin{abstract}
Labeling \LabeledDomains{} websites is a month of work; labeling \HeadlineBudget{} of them is an afternoon. On a metered browsing panel we show that the afternoon buys almost the same regression coefficients, provided the \HeadlineBudget{} are chosen by how much each site moves the estimate and weighted by the chance it had of being chosen. Sampling websites with probability proportional to influence attains \EffAtTwoFifty{} of the error that spending the same budget uniformly at random incurs, holds that ratio across a twentyfold range of budgets, and produces intervals that cover at their nominal rate where uniform sampling's cover at \SrsCoverageLo{}. The argument rests on an identity rather than an approximation: writing $V$ for row shares and $H=(X^\top X)^{-1}X^\top V$, the complete-label coefficient is $\beta^*=\sum_j h_j a_j$, so an item's entire contribution to the answer is fixed before any label is bought. From that identity we derive the optimal inclusion probabilities, show that the certainty head of a take-all design falls out of a constraint rather than a judgment call, give the take-some boundary in closed form, and show that the criterion has to be declared because fitted-value and coefficient loss can disagree on \LossDisagreementPct{} percent of a budget. We also show where the guarantee stops. When the purchased label builds a covariate rather than an outcome, an unbiased input yields an attenuated coefficient, and we give the correction the design makes available. The methods are implemented in an open-source package, and every number below regenerates from a hashed extract distributed with it.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

Some labels are expensive and get reused. A website's content category, a merchant's industry, a surname's likely ethnicity: each costs something to determine once, and then applies to every record touching that entity. A browsing panel of a thousand people generates millions of visits across tens of thousands of domains, and an analyst who wants to know whether older adults encounter more trackers must attach a label to the domains, not to the visits. The labeling bill scales with the number of distinct entities; the value of a label scales with how much of the data it touches and with what the analyst intends to estimate. Those two quantities are not the same, and the gap between them is the opportunity.

The usual response is to label as much as the budget allows, most valuable first, and treat what remains as zero. That is a reasonable instinct and it produces a biased estimate whose bias cannot be bounded from the data, because the unlabeled entities contribute an amount that depends on labels nobody bought. The alternative this paper develops is to treat the labeling decision as a sampling problem: give every entity that matters a positive and known chance of selection, choose those chances to minimize the variance of the estimate you actually want, and weight by the reciprocal.

That reframing is not new in outline. Business surveys have long taken all the large units and sampled the rest; case-cohort designs measure expensive covariates on every case plus a random subcohort \citep{Prentice1986}; high-influence probability sampling picks units by their anticipated contribution to a regression \citep{Clark2022}. What is specific here is that the purchased label attaches to an entity reused across many records through a map the analyst already knows, and that this makes the value of a label exact rather than anticipated. Writing $V$ for the matrix of row shares and $H=(X^\top X)^{-1}X^\top V$ with columns $h_1,\dots,h_m$, the complete-label regression coefficient decomposes with no remainder:
\[
\beta^*=\sum_{j=1}^m h_j a_j .
\]
Item $j$ contributes $h_j a_j$ and nothing else, and $h_j$ is computable from the usage counts and the covariates before a single label is purchased. Every result below is a consequence of that identity.

We contribute four things, of which the first three are propositions with short proofs rather than empirical findings. First, the designs: the risk-minimizing inclusion probabilities are $\pi_j^\star=\min\{1,\lambda\sqrt{b_j/c_j}\}$, in which the cap at one produces a certainty head rather than presuming one (Proposition~\ref{prop:pps}); the take-all boundary of the simpler uniform-tail design has a closed form that replaces a judgment call with arithmetic (Proposition~\ref{prop:core}); and when the trait is rare but its carriers unknown, the worst case remains a convex program (Proposition~\ref{prop:robust}). Second, the estimand must be declared: fitted-value loss and coefficient loss weight items differently and can disagree on \LossDisagreementPct{} percent of a budget, and there is a diagnostic for the populations on which the choice provably cannot matter. Third, the limits of the guarantee: reweighting is unbiased only under a condition that ordinary least squares meets and generalized least squares generally does not (Proposition~\ref{prop:unbiased}); when the label builds a covariate rather than an outcome, an unbiased constructed variable yields an attenuated coefficient, and the design's knowledge of its own error variance supplies the correction (Proposition~\ref{prop:attenuation}); and the reported standard error separates into a component the budget controls and one it cannot, whose ratio tells an analyst when to stop paying. Fourth, an open-source implementation \citep{fewlab}, in which the three objects this argument keeps distinct---a ranking, a probability design, and a realized sample---are distinct types, so the mistake of weighting a deterministic selection as though it were a sample cannot be expressed.

We evaluate on a population where the answer is known. A metered browsing panel of \PanelUsers{} US adults was scanned for tracking behavior, and \LabeledDomains{} of its \PanelDomains{} domains returned a successful scan. Restricting to those domains makes the complete-label coefficient exactly computable, so every design is scored against the same fixed target instead of against an estimate of one.

\section{Setup and an exact decomposition}

Let $C=(c_{ij})\in\mathbb{R}_+^{n\times m}$ be counts of unit $i$'s activity on item $j$, let $T_i=\sum_j c_{ij}$, and let $V_{ij}=c_{ij}/T_i$ be row shares. Item $j$ carries a latent label $a_j$, and the unit-level outcome is the share of activity on labeled items,
\[
y_i=\sum_{j=1}^m V_{ij}\,a_j .
\]
With covariates $X\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times p}$ the estimand is the complete-label regression coefficient $\beta^*=(X^\top X)^{-1}X^\top y$. Units with $T_i=0$ have no shares and are excluded.

\begin{proposition}[Exact decomposition]
\label{prop:decomposition}
Let $H=(X^\top X)^{-1}X^\top V$ with columns $h_1,\dots,h_m$. Then
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:decomposition}
\beta^*=\sum_{j=1}^m h_j a_j .
\end{equation}
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
$y=Va$ by definition, so $\beta^*=(X^\top X)^{-1}X^\top Va=Ha=\sum_j h_ja_j$, the last step being the definition of a matrix acting on a vector.
\end{proof}

The content is in what the statement does not contain. There is no error term, no distributional assumption, and no covariance to approximate: item $j$'s entire contribution to the estimand is $h_ja_j$, and $h_j$ depends only on $(C,X)$. The same three lines carry a vector label $a_j\in\mathbb{R}^q$, giving $\beta^*=\sum_jh_ja_j^\top$, so a mutually exclusive taxonomy and a coder's distribution over classes need no separate treatment.

Sample items with inclusion probabilities $\pi_j\in(0,1]$ and indicators $I_j$ that do not depend on the unknown labels, and write
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:ht}
\widehat\beta=\sum_{j=1}^m \frac{I_j}{\pi_j}h_j a_j
\end{equation}
for the Horvitz--Thompson estimator \citep{HorvitzThompson1952}.

\begin{proposition}[Unbiasedness, and its uniqueness]
\label{prop:unbiased}
$\E[\widehat\beta]=\beta^*$ whenever $\pi_j>0$ for every $j$ with $h_j\neq 0$. Conversely, a linear estimator $L\widehat y$ of $\beta^*$ built from the Horvitz--Thompson share vector $\widehat y$ is unbiased for every label configuration if and only if $LV=H$.
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
For the first claim, $\E[I_j/\pi_j]=1$ and everything else in \eqref{eq:decomposition} is fixed, so $\E[\widehat\beta]=\sum_jh_ja_j=\beta^*$; items with $h_j=0$ contribute nothing whatever $\pi_j$ is. For the second, $\E[\widehat y]=y=Va$, so $\E[L\widehat y]=LVa$ while $\beta^*=Ha$. These agree for all $a\in\mathbb{R}^m$ exactly when $LV=H$.
\end{proof}

Unbiasedness is therefore a constraint on the design rather than a property to hope for. The second half of the proposition is what rules out reweighting: $L=(X^\top X)^{-1}X^\top$ satisfies $LV=H$ by construction, and when $V$ has full row rank it is the only $L$ that does. Any other weighting, including the generalized least squares of \S\ref{sec:inference}, has to justify itself against a different estimand.

Two consequences deserve emphasis because they are what deterministic selection gives up. An item that no unit touched has $h_j=0$, contributes nothing, and may be left unlabeled at no cost in bias; this is the only case in which $\pi_j=0$ is correct. And selecting the top $K$ items by any criterion assigns probability one above the cut and zero below it, so its bias is $-\sum_{j\notin S}h_ja_j$, a quantity that depends on labels nobody purchased and therefore cannot be estimated or bounded from the data in hand.

\section{What an item is worth}

\begin{proposition}[Exact risk]
\label{prop:risk}
Under independent (Poisson) item sampling, and for positive semidefinite $W$, the loss $\tr[W(\widehat\beta-\beta^*)(\widehat\beta-\beta^*)^\top]$ has expectation
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:risk}
\E_p\,L=\sum_{j=1}^m \frac{1-\pi_j}{\pi_j}\,b_j,\qquad b_j=(h_j^\top W h_j)\,a_j^2 .
\end{equation}
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
By \eqref{eq:decomposition} and \eqref{eq:ht}, $\widehat\beta-\beta^*=\sum_j(I_j/\pi_j-1)h_ja_j$. The terms are independent with mean zero and $\Var(I_j/\pi_j)=(1-\pi_j)/\pi_j$, so the cross terms vanish and $\Cov(\widehat\beta)=\sum_j\frac{1-\pi_j}{\pi_j}a_j^2h_jh_j^\top$. Taking $\tr[W\,\cdot\,]$ and using $\tr[Wh_jh_j^\top]=h_j^\top Wh_j$ gives the claim.
\end{proof}

The criterion enters only through $b_j$, and it has to be chosen. Fitted-value loss sets $W=X^\top X$ and gives $g_j^\top(X^\top X)^{-1}g_j$ for $g_j=X^\top v_j$. Coefficient loss sets $W=I$ and gives $h_j^\top h_j=g_j^\top(X^\top X)^{-2}g_j$. A contrast $L^\top\beta^*$ sets $W=LL^\top$ and gives $\|L^\top h_j\|^2$. All three yield square-root allocations and convex programs. On a well-conditioned $X^\top X$ they rank items almost identically; they diverge when $X^\top X$ is far from a scaled identity and the $h_j$ span several directions, where in a deliberately ill-conditioned experiment they disagreed on \LossDisagreementPct{} percent of a top-$K$ set. There is no target-free notion of which items are worth labeling, and reporting one criterion while computing another is a mistake we have made ourselves.

\paragraph{Labels that need no pilot.} For a mutually exclusive taxonomy $a_j^\top a_j=1$, so $b_j=h_j^\top W h_j$ is known before any labeling begins and the optimal design can be computed from usage data alone. When labels are counts rather than class indicators the squared norms vary and a pilot sample or a proxy is required; \S\ref{sec:evidence} measures what that knowledge is worth.

\paragraph{When the criterion cannot matter.} If every $h_j$ points in the same direction and items differ only in magnitude, then $b_j\propto\|h_j\|^2$ under every $W$, all criteria induce the same ordering, and the choice of estimand is immaterial. Expected counts of the form $\text{activity}_i\times\text{popularity}_j$ produce exactly this degeneracy, and a simulation built on such a population will report every design as equivalent whatever it measures. The singular values of the column-normalized $H$ diagnose it, read against a noise floor of order $1/\sqrt{\bar c}$ for average count $\bar c$, since integer counts inject spurious directions at that scale.

\section{Designs}

\begin{proposition}[Optimal probabilities]
\label{prop:pps}
The minimizer of \eqref{eq:risk} subject to $\sum_jc_j\pi_j=K$ and $0<\pi_j\le1$ is
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:pps}
\pi_j^\star=\min\!\left\{1,\ \lambda\sqrt{b_j/c_j}\right\}
\end{equation}
for the unique $\lambda>0$ that exhausts the budget.
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
Dropping the constant $-\sum_jb_j$, the objective is $\sum_jb_j/\pi_j$, which is strictly convex on $\pi_j>0$; the feasible set is convex, so the Karush--Kuhn--Tucker conditions are necessary and sufficient. With multiplier $\nu$ on the budget and $\mu_j\ge0$ on $\pi_j\le1$, stationarity is $-b_j/\pi_j^2+\nu c_j+\mu_j=0$. Where the cap is slack, $\mu_j=0$ and $\pi_j=\sqrt{b_j/(\nu c_j)}$, which is \eqref{eq:pps} with $\lambda=\nu^{-1/2}$. Where it binds, $\pi_j=1$ and $\mu_j=b_j-\nu c_j\ge0$, which holds exactly when $\lambda\sqrt{b_j/c_j}\ge1$. The budget is continuous and strictly increasing in $\lambda$ until every $\pi_j$ is capped, so $\lambda$ is unique.
\end{proof} The cap is not a separate rule for large items: the set on which it binds \emph{is} the certainty head, and in a heavy-tailed population it absorbs most of the budget. The square-root rule also settles the unbiasedness constraint without further intervention, since it assigns strictly positive probability to every item with $b_j>0$. A probability floor is therefore unnecessary for unbiasedness, and imposing one moves the design away from minimum risk; its only legitimate purpose is to bound the largest weight.

\paragraph{A certainty core with a uniform tail.} Order the contributions $b_1\ge\cdots\ge b_m$, take the first $H$ with certainty, and give the remainder the common probability $p_H=(K-H)/(m-H)$.

\begin{proposition}[Closed-form core boundary]
\label{prop:core}
With $S_H=\sum_{j>H}b_j$, the Poisson risk of this design is
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:core}
R(H)=\frac{m-K}{K-H}\,S_H ,
\end{equation}
and $R(H+1)<R(H)$ if and only if $b_{H+1}>S_H/(K-H)$.
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
Core items have $\pi_j=1$ and contribute nothing to \eqref{eq:risk}. Each tail item contributes $\frac{1-p_H}{p_H}b_j$, and
\[
\frac{1-p_H}{p_H}=\frac{m-H-(K-H)}{K-H}=\frac{m-K}{K-H},
\]
so summing over the tail gives \eqref{eq:core}. For the second claim, $S_{H+1}=S_H-b_{H+1}$, and $R(H+1)<R(H)$ reads $(S_H-b_{H+1})(K-H)<S_H(K-H-1)$, which simplifies to $b_{H+1}(K-H)>S_H$.
\end{proof}

The boundary is therefore arithmetic rather than judgment: evaluate at most $K$ cutoffs, or apply the promotion rule directly. Two qualifications. Equation \eqref{eq:core} is exact for a Bernoulli tail; drawn as a fixed-size simple random sample the variance is smaller, because fixing the count removes the sample-size variability and contributions enter centered. Read it as a planning criterion and an upper bound. And the design cannot beat \eqref{eq:pps}, which varies the tail probabilities. Its appeal is operational: a uniform tail is a simple random sample, easy to specify to a vendor, easy to audit, and equipped with a variance estimator any reader can check.

\paragraph{When the labels are unknown but the trait is rare.} Both designs above need $b_j$, which contains $a_j^2$. For a mutually exclusive taxonomy that factor is one, but for a binary trait it requires knowing which items carry it. If instead one knows only that at most a fraction $\alpha$ of items are positive, the worst case is still tractable.

\begin{proposition}[Prevalence-robust design]
\label{prop:robust}
Write $t_j=\frac{1-\pi_j}{\pi_j}w_j$ for the label-free contribution and $M=\lceil\alpha m\rceil$. The largest risk over label configurations with at most $M$ positives is the sum of the $M$ largest $t_j$, which is a convex function of $\pi$; minimizing it subject to the budget is therefore a convex program. At $\alpha=1$ its solution is exactly \eqref{eq:pps} with unit label norms.
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
For $a_j\in\{0,1\}$ the risk is $\sum_ja_j^2t_j=\sum_{j:a_j=1}t_j$, maximized over configurations with at most $M$ positives by placing them on the $M$ largest terms. Convexity follows from the epigraph identity
\[
\sum_{k\le M}t_{(k)}=\min_{\tau}\Big\{M\tau+\sum_j\pos{t_j-\tau}\Big\}
\]
\citep[see][]{BoydVandenberghe2004}: each $\pos{t_j-\tau}$ is a nondecreasing convex function of the convex $t_j(\pi_j)=w_j/\pi_j-w_j$ and of $\tau$, hence jointly convex, and partial minimization over $\tau$ preserves convexity in $\pi$. At $M=m$ the sum of the $m$ largest terms is $\sum_jt_j$, so the objective becomes $\sum_jw_j/\pi_j-\sum_jw_j$ and Proposition~\ref{prop:pps} applies.
\end{proof}

The last sentence is worth stating because it is checkable: at full prevalence the robust design must reproduce the plain one exactly, not merely closely, and the accompanying test asserts equality to numerical precision for that reason.

\paragraph{Turning probabilities into a list.} A design is not a draw. Include every item with $\pi_j^\star=1$, then draw the remainder to exact size with first-order inclusions $\pi^\star$ and balancing auxiliaries $g_j$, using the cube method \citep{DevilleTille2004}. Balancing drives $\sum_j(I_j/\pi_j-1)g_j$ toward zero, which shrinks the realized error for any labels whatsoever, and costs nothing in unbiasedness because the first-order probabilities are unchanged. Estimation then uses the probabilities the sampler \emph{delivered}. If a sampler rescales the requested probabilities, as a fixed-size sampler must, the delivered vector is the one that makes \eqref{eq:ht} unbiased.

\paragraph{The reverse problem.} Fixing a per-unit precision instead of a budget, and minimizing the number of labels subject to $\sum_j q_{ij}/\pi_j\le\varepsilon_i^2+\sum_j q_{ij}$ for $q_{ij}=V_{ij}^2$, is convex with the same square-root shape $\pi_j^\star\propto\sqrt{\sum_i\mu_iq_{ij}/c_j}$. It is always feasible, since $\pi\equiv 1$ makes the design variance vanish.

\section{Using a cheap classifier}

Suppose a frozen classifier supplies a prediction $\mu_j$ for every item at negligible cost. The difference estimator
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:diff}
\widehat\beta_\mu=\sum_{j=1}^m h_j\mu_j+\sum_{j=1}^m\frac{I_j}{\pi_j}h_j(a_j-\mu_j)
\end{equation}
is design-unbiased whatever the classifier says, because the first term is a known constant and the second corrects it without bias. A poor classifier costs variance and never bias, so there is no risk in supplying one. The risk becomes $\sum_j\frac{1-\pi_j}{\pi_j}(h_j^\top Wh_j)\,\E(a_j-\mu_j)^2$, so acquisition should target influence times residual label uncertainty: for a calibrated binary probability $p_j$ that residual is $p_j(1-p_j)$, and for calibrated one-hot probabilities it is $1-\|\mu_j\|^2$, both known without labels. Plugging predictions directly into a regression instead is what produces the bias and overconfidence documented by \citet{FongTyler2021}; here the correction is made by the design rather than by a model of the prediction error.

\section{When the label builds a covariate}
\label{sec:covariate}

Everything above puts the purchased label in the outcome. Reverse it, and the central guarantee fails. Suppose the target is $\gamma$ in
\[
z_i=\gamma\,y_i+x_i^\top\delta+\epsilon_i
\]
where $y$ must again be constructed from labels. Substituting an unbiased $\widehat y=y+u$ regresses on a covariate measured with error, so $\gamma$ is \emph{attenuated} and not merely noisy. Unbiasedness of $\widehat y$ offers no protection, and the attenuation does not shrink with the size of the panel: it shrinks only with the labeling budget. On a synthetic population with $\gamma=\TrueGamma$ we measure a naive estimate of \NaiveGamma{}, an attenuation of \AttenuationPct{} percent.

What makes the problem tractable is that the measurement error was created deliberately.

\begin{proposition}[Attenuation, and its correction]
\label{prop:attenuation}
Let $M_X$ residualize on the controls and write $\widetilde y=M_X\widehat y$, $\widetilde z=M_Xz$. The naive slope $\widetilde y^\top\widetilde z/\widetilde y^\top\widetilde y$ has, to first order,
\[
\gamma\cdot\frac{y^\top M_Xy}{y^\top M_Xy+\E[u^\top M_Xu]}\ <\ \gamma ,
\qquad
\E[u^\top M_Xu]=\sum_j\frac{1-\pi_j}{\pi_j}a_j^2\,\bigl(v_j^\top M_Xv_j\bigr),
\]
and the attenuation factor depends on the labeling budget alone, not on the number of units.
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
Write $\widehat y=y+u$. The design is independent of $z$, so $\E[\widehat y^\top M_Xz]=y^\top M_Xz=\gamma\,y^\top M_Xy$ by the definition of $\gamma$ as the residualized slope. For the denominator, $\E[\widehat y^\top M_X\widehat y]=y^\top M_Xy+\E[u^\top M_Xu]$ because $u$ has mean zero and is independent of $y$. The stated expression is the ratio of these two, and it is strictly below $\gamma$ whenever $\E[u^\top M_Xu]>0$. Finally $\E[u^\top M_Xu]=\tr\bigl(M_X\Var(u)\bigr)$ with $\Var(u)=\sum_j\frac{1-\pi_j}{\pi_j}a_j^2v_jv_j^\top$, which gives the sum; every term is a function of $\pi$ and the labels, and none of $n$.
\end{proof}

The design chose $\pi$, so that inflation is known up to the labels and the sample estimates it without bias. Subtracting the estimate
\[
\widehat{\Delta}=\sum_{j\in S}\frac{1-\pi_j}{\pi_j^2}\,a_j^2\,\bigl(v_j^\top M_X v_j\bigr)
\]
removes the first-order attenuation. The correction is a ratio with a reduced denominator, so it overshoots: in the same experiment it moves an estimate that was \AttenuationPct{} percent low to one about \OvershootPct{} percent high. That is a large improvement and not an exact fix, and intervals should come from redrawing the design rather than from a closed form.

The design target changes too, and in an instructive direction. The outcome case rewards items whose exposure is \emph{aligned} with $X$, through $h_j^\top W h_j$. A covariate's coefficient is identified by the variation in $\widetilde y$ that survives residualizing, so the relevant quantity is $v_j^\top M_X v_j=\|v_j\|^2-h_j^\top X^\top X h_j$, the exposure \emph{orthogonal} to $X$. In principle these are complementary. In practice they are not far apart, because with many more units than covariates the orthogonal part dominates the squared norm; in this experiment the two scores correlate at \ScoreCorrelation{} and the better-targeted design improves root mean squared error by \RegressorGainPct{} percent. The lesson is the reverse of the outcome case: here the choice of design matters little and the failure to correct for attenuation matters a great deal.

\section{Inference}
\label{sec:inference}

\paragraph{Two variances, and only one is for sale.} Writing $\sigma^2$ for the residual variance of $y$ on $X$,
\[
\Var(\widehat\beta)=\underbrace{\sigma^2(X^\top X)^{-1}}_{\text{$n$ units}}+\underbrace{\textstyle\sum_j\frac{1-\pi_j}{\pi_j}b_j}_{\text{$K$ labels}} .
\]
Only the second term falls as the budget grows, so the ratio is a stopping rule: once the design term sits well below the first, further labels buy almost nothing and the binding constraint is the size of the panel. Estimating $\sigma^2$ requires care, because the residuals of $\widehat y$ on $X$ carry design noise as well as regression error. That noise contributes $\sum_j\frac{1-\pi_j}{\pi_j}a_j^2(\|v_j\|^2-h_j^\top X^\top Xh_j)$ to the residual sum of squares; subtracting it leaves an unbiased estimator rather than an inflated one.

\paragraph{Fixed-size draws are covered conservatively.} The Poisson expression \eqref{eq:risk} is exact for independent Bernoulli trials. Fixing the sample size and balancing on auxiliaries both remove variation it still counts, so a cube draw's true variance is smaller than the figure reported. Measured on the panel of \S\ref{sec:evidence}, the reported standard error exceeds the realized spread by \CubeSeExcess{} percent, pooled over coefficients across \CubeReps{} draws, and coverage runs to \CubeCoverage{} against a nominal $0.95$.

Two estimators recover what that conservatism costs, and they are the same construction with different things held fixed. Fixing the sample size removes the variation in how many items were drawn, and centring each contribution on a weighted mean of the others rather than on zero subtracts exactly that, which is the H\'ajek form for high-entropy unequal-probability designs \citep{Hajek1964}. Balancing removes more: a cube draw holds the auxiliary totals near their population values, so the part of a contribution the auxiliaries explain cannot vary either, and the residual from regressing on them is what remains \citep{DevilleTille2005}.

Fitting that regression consumes degrees of freedom, and how they are returned matters more than it appears. The usual correction is a shared factor $k/(k-q)$ for $q$ balancing constraints, which is exact when the residuals are homoskedastic. They are not: inclusion probabilities span orders of magnitude by construction, since that spread is the point of the design, and leverage spans them with it. Inflating each squared residual by its own leverage instead of by a shared factor moves the estimator from about four fifths of the true variance to within Monte Carlo error of it. Entities at certainty carry weight zero and no leverage, which is the correct treatment: they could not have been anywhere else. On a synthetic population where all three can be compared against the realized spread over many draws, the ratio of estimated to actual variance under a cube draw is \VarCubePoisson{} for the Poisson expression, \VarCubeHajek{} for H\'ajek and \VarCubeBalanced{} for the balanced residual; on a Bernoulli draw the same three read \VarPoissonPoisson{}, \VarPoissonHajek{} and \VarPoissonBalanced{}. Each is right for its own design and wrong for the others, so the estimator has to be named rather than inferred. Sen--Yates--Grundy estimators using exact joint inclusion probabilities would be exact rather than approximate \citep{Sen1953,YatesGrundy1953}, but those probabilities have no closed form for a cube draw.

\begin{remark}[Resampling does not rescue a bad design]
\label{rem:bootstrap}
It is tempting to answer a coverage shortfall with a bootstrap, and the
diagnosis above suggests why one might: skewness and an unstable variance
estimate are the conditions under which a bootstrap usually earns its keep. It
does not work here, and the reason is instructive. Under uniform sampling the
shortfall comes from draws that miss the high-influence items altogether. In
exactly those draws $\widehat\beta$ is far from $\beta^*$ \emph{and} the
estimated variance is small, because the only contributions available to
estimate a scale from are the small ones that were drawn. The error and its
estimated size are strongly dependent, and every bootstrap conditions on the
realized sample, so every bootstrap inherits that dependence. Resampling cannot
recover information about items that were never sampled.

Measured on a synthetic population, mean coverage of a nominal $0.95$ interval
under uniform sampling is \CovSrsNormal{} for the normal approximation,
\CovSrsRademacher{} for a wild bootstrap with Rademacher multipliers,
\CovSrsDesign{} for multipliers drawn from the design's own Bernoulli law, and
\CovSrsStudentized{} for the studentized bootstrap. All three variants are
calibrated to reproduce the design variance exactly, and all three are worse
than doing nothing. The studentized version, which is meant to offer a
higher-order refinement, is among the worst, because it leans harder on the
sample's own understated scale. Under clipped PPS the same four figures are
\CovPpsNormal{}, \CovPpsRademacher{}, \CovPpsDesign{} and
\CovPpsStudentized{}: little to fix and nothing gained. The remedy for a badly
allocated sample is a better allocation.
\end{remark}

\paragraph{Why item-clustered standard errors do not serve as a check.} Because the same items enter every unit's outcome, the errors of an ordinary least squares fit on $\widehat y$ are shared across units through items, which invites clustering by item as a conservative alternative. It is not one. The item-clustered meat matrix is $\sum_{j\in S}g_jg_j^\top a_j^2/\pi_j^2$ while the design meat is $\sum_{j\in S}(1-\pi_j)g_jg_j^\top a_j^2/\pi_j^2$, so the two differ term by term by $1/(1-\pi_j)$. Clustering treats the sampled items as draws from an infinite population and therefore discards the finite-population correction, charging full variance to the certainty core whose contribution is exactly zero. On the designs of \S\ref{sec:evidence} it overstates the variance by an order of magnitude or more, which is not conservatism but a different quantity.

\paragraph{A different estimand, and the one weighting that suits it.} Proposition~\ref{prop:unbiased} rules out reweighting only for the finite-population target. If the units are a sample from a population in which $y=X\beta+\epsilon$, then $\beta$ rather than $\beta^*$ is the object, $\widehat y$ has error variance $\sigma^2I+\Var(u)$, and that variance is markedly heteroskedastic: a unit whose activity concentrates on the certainty core carries almost no design noise, while a unit spread across obscure items carries a great deal. Generalized least squares exploits exactly that, and is unusually safe here because $\Var(u)$ is known from the design rather than estimated from residuals. It is also, by Proposition~\ref{prop:unbiased}, biased for $\beta^*$: on a synthetic population its variance is smaller by a factor of six to sixteen and its bias for the finite-population coefficient is ten standard errors. Neither number is a defect. They are what it means for two estimators to answer different questions, and the choice has to be made before either is reported.

\paragraph{Buying bias on purpose.} Shrinking the weight so item $j$ enters with $\alpha_j/\pi_j$ is weight trimming \citep{Potter1990,Beaumont2013}. It is defensible here because the price is computable in advance: the bias is bounded by $\sum_j(1-\alpha_j)\|h_j\|\,|a_j|$, which for a mutually exclusive taxonomy needs no labels, and which the classifier of \eqref{eq:diff} converts from a bound into an estimate. Setting $\alpha_j=\pi_j$, so every labeled item enters with weight one and unlabeled items are treated as zeros, is the common practice of zero-filling; it is a point on this frontier and \S\ref{sec:evidence} finds it is never the best one.

\section{Evidence}
\label{sec:evidence}

Propositions~\ref{prop:decomposition} through \ref{prop:attenuation} are exact, so nothing below is needed to establish them. What simulation supplies is the two things proof does not: how large these quantities are on a population somebody actually has, and whether the implementation computes what the algebra says. Where a proposition makes a sharp prediction, the check is written as an equality rather than an inequality, so that a correct implementation is distinguished from a merely plausible one.

We evaluate on a population whose labels are entirely known, so that every design is scored against the same fixed target rather than against an estimate of one. The panel is YouGov Pulse: \PanelUsers{} US adults metered for June 2022, \PanelVisits{} visits across \PanelDomains{} registered domains \citep{PulseData}. Each domain was submitted to Blacklight for a seven-measure tracking audit, and \LabeledDomains{} scans succeeded, covering \LabelCoverage{} percent of domains and \LabeledVisitShare{} percent of visits \citep{BlacklightData}. The evaluation frame is those domains, and it is \EvalDensity{} percent dense.

The outcome is the published per-user exposure rate, which is exactly $y=Va$, and the regression is the published specification of exposure on gender, race, education, and age group. Fitting it on all \LabeledDomains{} labels reproduces the published coefficients to within \ReproMaxDiff{} against a stated replication tolerance of \ReproTolerance{}, which establishes that the exercise targets the same quantity the substantive paper does. With \NumClasses{} label measures reduced to the two headline ones, $\beta^*$ is $\BetaRows\times\BetaCols$ and $\|\beta^*\|=\TruthNorm$. Each row of Table~\ref{tab:evidence} is \Reps{} independent Poisson draws from the same finite population, so the reported spread is design variance alone.

Every design is unbiased, including the poor one. Uniform sampling's bias at $K=250$ sits within a Monte Carlo standard error of zero. The designs differ in variance and not in bias, which is what a probability design guarantees and what separates this exercise from zero-filling, whose bias is real and unbounded.

The efficiency ratio is flat in the budget. Clipped PPS holds between \EffRatioLo{} and \EffRatioHi{} of the uniform error across a twentyfold range of $K$, so the gain is a property of the design rather than of a fortunate operating point. At $K=2{,}000$ its error is \RmseAtTwoThousand{}, or \RelErrAtTwoThousand{} percent of the size of the quantity being estimated, from labeling \BudgetShareAtTwoThousand{} percent of what was actually labeled.

Intervals from a good design cover and intervals from a poor one do not. Uniform sampling covers at \SrsCoverageLo{} to \SrsCoverageHi{} against a nominal $0.95$, and the cause is not a faulty variance estimator: over repeated draws it recovers the true variance to a ratio of \SrsVarRatio{}. What fails is the normal approximation. Under uniform sampling a few very high influence domains either enter the sample or do not, leaving $\widehat\beta$ skewed at \SrsSkew{} with a variance estimate whose own standard deviation is \SrsVarDispersion{} times its mean; under PPS those figures are \PpsSkew{} and \PpsVarDispersion{}. The design that shrinks the interval is also what makes the interval mean anything, and reporting a standard error from a badly allocated sample is the more dangerous of the two failures because it looks like an answer.

Knowing the squared label norms is worth a further factor of \OracleFactor{}. Blacklight labels are counts rather than class indicators, with squared norms running from a median of \NormMedian{} to a maximum of \NormMax{}, so the simplification $a_j^\top a_j=1$ does not apply. That gap measures what a pilot sample or the classifier of \eqref{eq:diff} is worth on this population, and it is the largest single improvement available.

\section{Related work}

The geometry of a certainty head with a sampled tail is old. Business surveys take all the large units and probability-sample the rest; case-cohort designs collect expensive covariates for every case plus a random subcohort \citep{Prentice1986}; high-influence probability sampling selects units by their contribution to the eventual regression estimator, with Poisson probabilities proportional to the square root of anticipated squared influence \citep{Clark2022}. The square-root allocation itself goes back to optimal experimental design \citep{Kiefer1959} and, for allocation across strata, to \citet{Neyman1934}. Our contribution is not that shape but its exactness: because the purchased label attaches to an entity reused through a known map, \eqref{eq:decomposition} makes an item's contribution a computed quantity rather than an anticipated one, which is what turns the certainty boundary into arithmetic and makes the choice of criterion consequential.

Two adjacent literatures supply the machinery. Two-phase validation designs optimize a subsample of \emph{units} to estimate regression parameters under cost constraints, often through influence-function-weighted allocation \citep{ChenLumley2022,McIsaacCook2015}; we optimize a subsample of \emph{items} used to construct the outcome. Balanced sampling and calibration match auxiliary totals and reduce realized variance without changing first-order inclusions \citep{DevilleSarndal1992,DevilleTille2004}, which is exactly what \S4 asks of the cube draw. Randomized sketching offers a useful contrast: leverage-score methods sample rows of $X$ to approximate a least-squares fit on data already in hand \citep{DrineasMahoney2016}, whereas we sample columns in order to construct the outcome at all. Finally, \S\ref{sec:covariate} connects to work on machine predictions used as regression inputs \citep{FongTyler2021}; that literature corrects an estimator downstream of a fixed prediction, while the design here chooses which predictions to replace with truth.

\section*{Software}

The methods are implemented in the open-source Python package \texttt{fewlab} \citep{fewlab}. Its API keeps apart the three objects this argument distinguishes. A \texttt{Study} holds $(C,X)$ and computes $H$; \texttt{Study.design} returns inclusion probabilities under a declared loss, with \texttt{robust\_pps} for the prevalence-bounded case of Proposition~\ref{prop:robust}; \texttt{Study.draw} realizes them by Poisson trials or a balanced cube draw; and \texttt{Study.estimate} returns $\widehat\beta$ with both variance components and a choice of variance estimator matched to the draw. The two estimators that answer different questions live in their own modules and say so: \texttt{estimate\_share\_effect} for a label-built covariate, and \texttt{estimate\_superpopulation} for the generalized least squares target. Only a realized draw is accepted by the estimator, since a ranking carries no inclusion probabilities and cannot be weighted; that constraint is enforced by the type rather than by documentation. Counts may be supplied in long format, one row per unit and item, which is how panels arrive and what keeps a matrix of the density reported in \S\ref{sec:evidence} from being materialized.

Every number in Tables~\ref{tab:evidence} and \ref{tab:frontier} and in Figure~\ref{fig:efficiency} is produced by \texttt{benchmarks/make\_exhibits.py} from a hashed extract distributed with the repository, in which panelist identifiers and domain names are replaced by salted digests. See \url{https://github.com/finite-sample/fewlab}.

\section*{Assumptions and limits}

Labels are assumed accurate once observed; under misclassification the estimator targets the noisy trait, and a correction is required. Item selection may depend on $(C,X)$ but not on the unknown labels, which holds by construction when $\pi$ is fixed before labeling and fails under multi-wave designs unless the final inclusion probabilities reflect the adaptivity. If $X^\top X$ is ill-conditioned, a ridge term keeps $H$ well defined at the cost of changing the estimand to the corresponding ridge coefficient. The variance formulas assume Poisson sampling and are conservative for fixed-size draws. And the evaluation frame consists of the domains whose scan succeeded, which is not a random subset of all domains; the exercise establishes what a design recovers relative to labeling that frame exhaustively, not what exhaustive labeling of the full population would have shown.
